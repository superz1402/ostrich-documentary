# TREATMENT — The Ostrich, Plate VII

**Style:** Copperplate Engraving (`styles/engraving`)
**Format:** 1920×1080 · 24 fps · ~58 s · English narration (British naturalist, Kokoro `bm_george`) · burned script subtitles + .srt · −14 LUFS
**Subject:** a documentary on the ostrich, told as a natural-history plate that engraves itself.

## 1. Three candidate structures

**A. The book of plates.** Four small plates turned like book pages, one fact each (speed, eye, egg, range map). Clean and modular, but each page re-sets the composition; the bird never accumulates into one finished figure, and the 8 s/plate rhythm fights a 58 s runtime.

**B. States of the plate.** One view of the bird re-cut three times — day bird, night bird (male incubating), chick — burnishing out and re-cutting regions. Strong craft story, but the documentary facts (size, speed, egg) would arrive out of order and the "before/after" turn belongs to a place, not an animal.

**C. One plate, one build (CHOSEN).** A single finished zoological plate assembles over the film in real engraving order — first cut, outline, three passes of hatching, labels, roundel, proof, hand colour. Every fact is attached to the layer that carries it; the film ends where a museum plate ends: title, scientific name, credits, locked. The bookend: the film opens mid-cut on bare copper with "It cannot fly" and closes on the finished plate with "It never needed to."

Why C: the museum-plate use case is the style's native grammar for ordered information; one accumulating plate gives continuous forward motion (no resets), every native move (the cut, the three passes, the magnification roundel, the proof, hand colouring, natural size) has a fact to serve, and the "finished plate" ending is an echo of the opening cut.

## 2. Logline and arc

A bird that cannot fly is engraved onto copper, line by line, until the plate that proves it never needed to is finished.

**Arc:** setup (a first stroke on bare copper — what is this bird?) → escalation (the plate builds: size, then speed, then the eye's paradox — the film's turn, where colour enters for the only time) → resolution (the proof is pulled, colour blooms on the egg, the plate locks complete).

## 3. Benchmark

- **Learn:** 19th-century zoological plate composition grammar (Audubon, Haeckel, the *Britannica* copperplates) — centred figure inside the plate mark, numbered detail roundels in the margins, roman title over italic scientific name, "del." / "sculp." credits; BBC natural-history narration cadence (fact, fact, wonder, fact); the engraving engine's own grammar (swell law, tone thresholds, wash blooms) as techniques only.
- **Do not take:** the honeybee demo's story, shot order, roundel count, plate layout, props or timeline; any actual plate, pose, border or title from historical works; no narration wording from any existing documentary.

## 6. Cue map

Tempo: **88 bpm**, 4/4, beat = 0.6818 s; 85 beats ≈ 57.95 s. Score: harpsichord (VCSL), lute (pluck `nylon_guitar`, physical model), harp (VCSL, only after the turn), recorder (VCSL, outro only), triangle `muted` tings for roundel events. ## 8. Subtitles and titles

- Narration subtitles: **Pinyon Script** on a deckled paper slip, bottom centre, ≤ 2 lines, never over a margin label; hold ≥ max(1.8 s, speech + 0.6 s).
- Plate lettering: **Bodoni Moda** roman capitals, cut glyph by glyph (title ~50 px wide tracking; labels 15–19 px); italics for *Struthio camelus*; script (Pinyon) for the "del." / "sculp." note, written in one stroke. Nothing fades.
- No opening title card; the title belongs to the finished plate at 48 s.

## 9. Colour reserve

Monochrome ink until 33.5 s. Amber iris inside the roundel (only colour on the bird). Cream eggshell + a madder shadow in the egg cut-away after the proof. The paper, copper and inks stay uncoloured throughout.
